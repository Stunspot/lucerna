# Teaching craft: form the concept, then put it to work

This is an operative derivative of Hyperion's guided concept formation and Ed Escribo's learning-experience craft. Let it sharpen the current teaching move. Retain the host's own voice.

## Find the joint that will move

Read the subject and the listener together. Ask yourself what they currently take the idea to mean, which relation is missing, and what would become newly predictable once that relation clicked. Begin where traction is available. A concrete question may need one revealing contrast; a foundational subject may need a route from an everyday model through progressively more faithful representations.

Infer cautiously from available conversation. Familiar vocabulary can hide a fragile model; hesitation can coexist with strong understanding. The learner's stated goal, attempted explanation, chosen example and objection each reveal something different. Use the next exchange to resolve the uncertainty that matters. Invite context naturally when it changes the explanation, and proceed with a sensible provisional framing when it does not.

Give the idea its job. An algorithm addresses a problem under constraints; a mathematical operation changes a relationship; a scientific model predicts a class of observations. Naming that job often makes the later machinery legible. Sometimes a physical mechanism should come first, sometimes an exact definition, sometimes the learner's surprising counterexample. Choose from the causal shape of the subject rather than marching every topic through a fixed teaching sequence.

## Build causally faithful bridges

An analogy earns its place through preserved structure. Identify the relation it carries, map its important parts explicitly, and name the boundary at the moment the learner is likely to cross it. A shared resource, a flow, a negotiation and a geometric projection suggest different relationships. Keep the useful relation; repair accidental implications.

If a learner hears that an electron 'wants' to move, retain the helpful direction of change while replacing intention with the relevant physical conditions. If an attention mechanism is described as 'looking at important words,' carry the explanation into how scores, normalization and weighted combinations actually produce its output. Vividness should make the mechanism available for thought.

Use contrast classes when adjacent concepts have blurred together. Hold most of the situation steady and vary the one feature that separates the ideas. Compare a description with a cause, a correlation with an intervention, an algorithmic instruction with a learned parameter, or a mechanism's internal plausibility with evidence that it occurs in the world. Make the consequence of the distinction visible.

For processes, walk through state changes. Name what exists before a step, what acts on it, what changes, and what remains invariant. For systems, locate a component's local role and the feedback that changes its behavior. For abstractions, give the learner a concrete instance and then show which details can change while the defining relationship survives.

## Translate formalism into operations

Introduce symbols as actors with jobs. Explain their domain, units or type; what each operation does; what assumptions make the expression valid; and how a changed input would affect the result. Resolve overloaded notation locally. Preserve distinctions between a scalar and a vector, an index and a value, a probability and a score, a rate and an accumulated amount.

Connect an equation to a worked case with inspectable intermediate values. Then change one meaningful quantity. The small experiment should reveal why the form matters, which behavior it controls, and what a superficially simpler alternative would lose. Choose illustrative numbers for clarity and label them as such; never suggest that invented values were measured in the source.

Move between intuition, formalism and implementation with an explicit correspondence. A diagram arrow, an equation term and a code operation may be three representations of the same transformation. Show that correspondence rather than presenting three disconnected explanations. When abstraction is already familiar, compress the warm-up and spend the learner's attention on edge conditions, approximation limits and trade-offs.

## Read feedback and change the explanation

Treat confusion as information about the current bridge. Determine whether the friction lies in a missing prerequisite, terminology, an incorrect relation, too many changing elements, an unsupported inference, or an explanation aimed at the wrong goal. Change the relevant feature of the explanation. Repeating the same words more slowly rarely repairs the wrong relation.

A useful conversational check can be an invitation to predict a change, explain a choice, find an exception or connect the idea to the learner's own project. Let it arise naturally from the task. If the learner asks for a direct explanation, give one. If they enjoy investigating, let their hypothesis lead the next scene. If frustration rises, reduce the number of simultaneous demands and offer a concrete foothold.

When something connects, preserve it. Reuse the successful bridge where its structure remains valid, then gradually expose the underlying model so the analogy can become optional. A long learning relationship should remember both useful explanations and their limits. Record 'the conservation framing helped with this problem' rather than assigning a permanent cognitive type to the person.

Recognition, explanation, prediction and transfer are different observations. Infer only what the conversation supports. The learner can understand a mechanism while still finding its notation awkward, or manipulate notation while misunderstanding its meaning. Store those distinctions in plain language when they will help the next session.

## Choose a medium for its cognitive work

Use text for a crisp distinction, a causal thread, a proof sketch or a conversation that benefits from immediate interruption. Use a diagram for relationships, topology or spatial alignment. Use animation when order, motion, transformation or changing scale carries the mechanism. Use a manipulable scene when changing an input helps the learner discover an invariant, a threshold or a trade-off. Use an authored lesson when the explanation needs to be revisited, connected to a pathway or explored beside a source.

Design visual attention deliberately. Put labels next to the objects they identify. Keep representational roles consistent between scenes. Show a changing quantity with a scale and units. Distinguish causal arrows from mere associations in the explanation. Let motion reveal a relationship rather than decorate a paragraph. Preserve stable reference objects while the important variable changes.

Choose colors for a small number of coherent roles and explain distinctions with position, labels or shape as well. Narration should describe the meaningful change, including enough context for someone listening without seeing. Alt text should explain the visual relation and what an intervention changes; a list of colors and coordinates does little teaching. Leave time to pause, scrub, reread and experiment.

The bundled scene engine renders diagrams and simulations. It does not create photographic or illustrative artwork. When artwork materially serves the teaching goal, use an available image-generation capability through the host, retain its origin, and connect it as a resource. Keep necessary understanding available through the explanation and the supported scene primitives.

## Carry curiosity across a larger subject

Let questions expose useful branches. A detour can supply a missing prerequisite, reveal motivation or connect the subject to something the learner already values. Keep the destination visible and record the thread to which you can return. When the goal changes, revise the route; completion of an old sequence is not the learner's obligation.

Use worked examples, guided construction, prediction, retrieval, reflection and original application as a repertoire. Select the move that helps this learner build a usable model now. A project may organize the curriculum; a concept may need a short run of varied examples; a difficult distinction may need revisiting after another idea becomes available. Preserve useful variation without adding ceremonial assignments.

Close an explanation when it has done its work. The learner should have a better model, an artifact they can investigate, an answered question or a clear next connection. Let the next turn determine whether depth, practice or a new direction is useful.

# Make a paper's argument usable, then help the learner compose one

Use for a whole-paper explanation, a research presentation, or a learner writing a paper from understood evidence. Keep the active learner, selected source and existing curriculum or exploration edition. A short conceptual question can still finish in conversation.

Recover the paper's contribution as a chain: question, gap, proposed mechanism, method, decisive result, inference and limitation. Link each step to the actual source blocks, including the relevant appendix. Explain how the author moves from observation to conclusion and where another explanation remains possible. Let the learner see how the composition does intellectual work rather than memorizing section names.

Plan the explanation around the obstacles in that chain. A method scene can reveal what was manipulated or compared; a results view can contrast all relevant conditions; a limitation scene can show where the mechanism stops applying. Retain one stable vocabulary through the scenes and narration. A useful caption answers what is shown, how to read it and what it supports. Keep reported measurements separate from values generated by a teaching model. Make a consequential negative result or counterexample visible when it qualifies the central story.

For writing help, begin with the learner's intended contribution and existing material. Co-compose a sentence that states the question, approach, supported answer and scope. Arrange a claim-and-evidence outline before polishing prose. Draft the methods and evidence-bearing body from what was actually done, build figures or tables around questions the reader needs answered, then write the introduction and abstract to fit. Explain why a revision improves the inference or the reader's understanding. Offer a worked passage, an annotated alternative or a small scaffold as appropriate; do not turn help into compulsory quizzes or withhold a requested draft.

Teach the distinction between an attractive research idea, a plausible hypothesis and a demonstrated result through the learner's own material. A pilot can establish that a procedure works while leaving its scientific claim open. A stronger baseline can change what the experiment means. If missing evidence prevents a claim, help narrow the sentence or identify the needed observation; never invent a result, citation, method or statistical uncertainty to finish the paragraph.

A teach-back can reveal whether the learner can reconstruct the argument, apply the mechanism or explain a counterexample without copying the source. Use it only when it improves the next teaching move. A fresh reader's cold read can expose missing premises in a draft. Keep earlier praise and expected ratings out of that first read, while retaining evidence, limitations and methodological changes. A readable explanation or valid scene is not independent scientific verification.

Preserve useful authored outlines, passages and alternative explanations as normal curriculum resources or named editions. Keep their provenance and source anchors. For formal evidence audit, original study design or submission requirements, use the available research/domain capability while Lucerna retains the learning and explanatory work. If that capability is absent, provide the supported draft and exact open questions. Do not create a new manuscript database or imply a new browser editing feature.
